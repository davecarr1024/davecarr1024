# Castles And Warfare In The Middle Ages

## Research Identity

The familiar Dover title *Castles and Warfare in the Middle Ages* republishes
M. Macdermott's 1860 translation, *An Essay on the Military Architecture of
the Middle Ages*. The work is a continuous illustrated argument rather than a
modern typological handbook.

Where *Annals of a Fortress* tests successive works in one fictional place,
this essay moves among real and reconstructed European examples to explain
why military architecture changed. It supplies the component vocabulary and
comparative cases needed to make the chronicle generalizable.

## Shape Of The Essay

The essay progresses roughly through these lenses:

| Lens | Representative material | Model relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inherited Roman practice | Camps, town walls, towers, Visigothic adaptation | Existing fabric and copied technique |
| Timber and early enclosure | Wooden ramparts and temporary works | Material, labor, fire, and rapid construction |
| Siege mechanisms | Testudo, ram, engines, mine, protected approach | Attacker capabilities and work counters |
| Projection and flanking | Towers, bastions, wall appendages | Visibility, wall-foot defense, and mutual support |
| Feudal castle | Keeps, baileys, concentrated lordly defense | Protected assets, fallback, autonomy, and control |
| Comparative plans | Paris, Château-Gaillard, Coucy, Provins, Carcassonne, Aigues-Mortes | Reusable geometric patterns grounded in examples |
| Entrances and wall operation | Gates, drawbridges, shutters, hoardings, machicolation | Access state, vertical action, and staged breach |
| Changes in warfare | Infantry, discipline, longbow, crossbow, siege narratives | Actor and weapon profiles |
| Gunpowder transition | Cannon, embrasures, altered towers and old works | Retrofit, obsolescence, and changing geometry |
| Earth and artillery works | Gabions, trenches, boulevards, cavaliers, orillons, bastions | Cover, batteries, approach, and overlapping fire |

The printed contents are unusually granular. Individual headings often name a
single mechanism, structure, example, or transition. That makes the book well
suited to a structured topic index even though its argument is continuous.

## The Core Analytical Posture

### Architecture as a response, not a style

Viollet-le-Duc treats architectural form as the residue of a tactical problem.
A tower projects because defenders need lateral action. A wall grows thicker
or lower because artillery changes damage. An entrance bends or accumulates
barriers because direct access is dangerous. Earth and timber appear where
their absorption, repairability, availability, or construction speed is useful.

Project implication: model works as bundles of capabilities and liabilities,
not as visual archetypes.

### The attacker is present in every plan

Even static plans are explained through anticipated assault. The relevant
questions are always relational:

- Where can the attacker stand or work?
- What protects his movement?
- Which surface can he strike, mine, burn, or breach?
- Which defenders can see or act against him?
- What must he do before bringing the next weapon forward?
- What happens when one work is suppressed or captured?

Project implication: every defensive analysis should name the attacker
capability and approach against which its claim is valid.

### Change is often retrofit before replacement

The essay repeatedly describes old works being thickened, pierced, lowered,
backed with earth, furnished with new platforms, or surrounded by new works.
Fortification evolves through accumulated constraints rather than clean-sheet
design alone.

Project implication: a commission must distinguish inherited immutable work,
modifiable work, removable work, and new construction. Generating a plan on an
empty field would miss one of the books' richest problems.

## Mechanism Families

### Site and approach

Relevant variables include:

- elevation and commanding ground;
- escarpment and slope;
- river, moat, marsh, and ditch;
- road, bridge, and gate alignment;
- exposed and masked ground;
- space available for engines, batteries, camps, and trenches;
- routes for supply, reinforcement, sortie, and retreat.

These suggest separating a route's physical possibility from its tactical
quality. An approach may be traversable while slow, visible, confined,
enfiladed, difficult to supply, or unable to carry a particular engine.

### Enclosure, curtain, and projection

A bare enclosure resists entry but can leave its base masked from defenders.
Projecting towers and later bastions create lateral or oblique action and help
neighboring segments.

Useful geometric predicates include:

- can observe;
- can engage;
- commands the foot of;
- flanks;
- is masked by;
- is exposed from;
- overlaps fire with;
- supports;
- is itself supported by.

The domain meaning of these predicates requires weapon, height, opening,
orientation, range, and obstruction information. Geometry is necessary but not
sufficient.

### Gate and controlled passage

Entrances concentrate the conflict between access and denial. The essay's
gates, drawbridges, passages, towers, barbicans, shutters, and successive doors
suggest a stateful passage rather than one wall opening.

A useful gate model may include:

- allowed direction and width;
- open, closed, raised, barred, or destroyed state;
- cover and exposure by segment;
- attacker orientation and shield assumptions;
- capacity for friendly sortie and retreat;
- sequence of barriers;
- supporting fire from adjacent works;
- fallback after an outer barrier fails.

### Keep and internal independence

The keep is not merely a large tower. In the essay's logic it concentrates
resistance, command, storage, habitation, and final refuge. Its value depends
on access, internal circulation, water or supplies, relation to the enclosure,
and whether loss of the bailey compromises it.

This suggests evaluating a plan as a sequence of defensible states rather than
only measuring its external perimeter.

### Mine and countermine

Mining turns geology, foundations, concealment, labor, and time into an attack
path. Countermining adds uncertain observation and interception.

Model concepts include:

- feasible subterranean volume;
- starting cover;
- excavation rate and support cost;
- target foundation;
- acoustic or other detection region;
- uncertainty in estimated enemy heading;
- interception or camouflet options;
- collapse and breach effects.

### Shielded and prepared approach

Testudos, mantelets, galleries, trenches, gabions, earthworks, and saps all
change exposure by allowing work or movement under protection. They should not
be reduced to a generic armor bonus.

Useful effects include:

- blocks a class of observation or projectile;
- protects only specified directions;
- reduces movement or construction speed;
- requires suitable ground and width;
- exposes workers during setup;
- enables a new action at its endpoint;
- can be burned, crushed, enfiladed, or counter-battered.

### Fire artillery and transition

The arrival and improvement of cannon do not flip a single technology flag.
They change:

- effective engagement distance;
- breaching power and preferred target;
- useful wall profile and material;
- space needed for a battery;
- recoil, platform, ammunition, and embrasure requirements;
- exposure of high masonry;
- value of earth backing and repair;
- spacing and shape of projecting works;
- viability of inherited towers and gates.

A period profile should therefore carry interacting capabilities rather than a
year-to-bonus lookup table.

### Bastion, demi-lune, boulevard, and cavalier

The later portions show medieval forms moving toward artillery fortification.
The significant idea is not a catalog of star-shaped polygons. It is arranging
works so that:

- likely attack fronts are observed and engaged from multiple positions;
- dead ground is reduced;
- an attacker must suppress several supporting works;
- outer works keep batteries away from the main enclosure;
- defenders retain protected movement and elevated or indirect action;
- damage to one face does not automatically silence its neighbors.

This is the natural source foundation for Barbacane's eventual overlapping-fire
and demi-lune studies.

## Geometry In The Illustrations

The essay uses plans, elevations, sections, and perspective details for
different explanatory jobs:

- plans explain access, adjacency, projection, and fire relationship;
- sections explain height, thickness, cover, ditch, and vertical circulation;
- elevations explain openings, machinery, and exposed surfaces;
- detail drawings explain construction and operation;
- comparative figures show how one response creates the next counter-response.

Barbacane reports should retain this plurality. A single overhead heat map
cannot adequately explain mines, wall profiles, covered movement, gate
machinery, or artillery protection.

## Design Vocabulary Worth Preserving

The following terms are especially useful when they become defined model
concepts:

- approach;
- enceinte and curtain;
- tower;
- keep or donjon;
- gate and postern;
- barbican;
- bailey;
- ditch, escarp, and counterscarp;
- hoarding and machicolation;
- mine and countermine;
- breach and retrenchment;
- braie and fausse-braie;
- boulevard;
- bastion and orillon;
- cavalier;
- demi-lune;
- gabion, trench, sap, and battery;
- embrasure, parapet, and traverse;
- enfilade, flanking, masking, and command.

Terms should enter code only after the project defines their geometry,
preconditions, effects, or diagnostic role.

## Limits And Cautions

- The book is a nineteenth-century synthesis of a broad and regionally varied
  period.
- It often reconstructs how damaged monuments once worked.
- Drawings may communicate the author's rational interpretation rather than an
  uncontested archaeological state.
- The work can make development appear more linear and functionally inevitable
  than it was.
- Social organization, supply, finance, disease, negotiation, and ordinary
  civilian experience receive less systematic treatment than architectural
  and military mechanism.
- Weapon ranges and effects should not be calibrated from casual prose or
  diagram scale without corroboration.
- The Dover title can obscure the edition lineage; citations should identify
  whether they refer to Dover or the 1860 Macdermott edition.

These limitations strengthen rather than weaken its project use. The book
offers explicit hypotheses that an executable model can expose, qualify, and
compare.

## Questions For Executable Research

- Can the system derive the need for projection from a wall-foot visibility
  failure?
- Can it distinguish flanking, enfilading, convergent, and merely overlapping
  fire?
- Can a gate design be evaluated as a sequence of passage states?
- Can shielded approach be modeled as directional protection with operational
  cost rather than generic damage reduction?
- Can inherited masonry be adapted under a new artillery profile more cheaply
  than it can be replaced?
- Can an agent decide when a keep adds meaningful independence and when it only
  traps resources?
- Can a mine and countermine report explain uncertain inference as clearly as
  surface geometry?
- Can a generated plan use the vocabulary correctly because the works solve
  represented problems, rather than because a period template says they
  should appear?
